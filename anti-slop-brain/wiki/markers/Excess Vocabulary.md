# Excess Vocabulary

Kobak and colleagues did not build a detector. They counted words. The method
in `kobak-excess-vocabulary` takes word frequencies across more than 15 million
PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024, extrapolates from the pre-2023 trend what
each word's 2024 frequency should have been, and measures the gap. Words whose
observed frequency vastly exceeds the extrapolation are "excess" words. No
classifier is trained, no document is scored, and no individual paper is
accused of anything. That design is why this marker sits at Tier 1 in
[[Evidence Tiers]] while most vocabulary claims sit at Tier 3.

## The figures, and the ones that are wrong

The correct figures are: **at least 13.5 percent** of 2024 abstracts show signs
of LLM processing, rising to **up to 40 percent** in some subcorpora. The
source is Science Advances 11(27), published 2025-07-02, DOI
10.1126/sciadv.adt3813.

The pair you will see everywhere else is 10 percent and 30 percent. Those
numbers come from the superseded June 2024 preprint version of the same work.
They are superseded, not merely rounded, and this brain treats quoting them as
a citation defect rather than a rounding difference. The correction is recorded
in [[Superseded Figures]] rather than applied silently, so that a reader
arriving with the old numbers finds out what happened to them.

| Figure | Status | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| at least 13.5 percent of 2024 abstracts | current | Science Advances 11(27), 2025-07-02 |
| up to 40 percent in some subcorpora | current | same paper |
| 10 percent | superseded | superseded June 2024 preprint |
| 30 percent | superseded | superseded June 2024 preprint |

## What the excess words actually are

The finding that matters for repair is not the headline percentage. It is the
shape of the excess: the excess words are overwhelmingly **stylistic verbs and
adjectives**, not content words. Nothing new was being said in 2024 abstracts.
The same claims arrived wearing different clothes.

The cohort documented in `wikipedia-signs-of-ai-writing`, which overlaps
heavily with the corpus finding, currently reads:

| Grammatical role | Cohort members |
| --- | --- |
| verbs | delve, underscore, highlight, showcase, enhance, foster, garner, align with, bolster, emphasize |
| adjectives | crucial, pivotal, key, intricate, robust, meticulous, enduring, vibrant, valuable |
| abstract nouns | landscape, tapestry, testament, interplay, realm |
| connectives | additionally, moreover, furthermore |

Two disciplines apply to this list. First, it is to be read literally: a word
being overused does not imply its synonyms are overused, so "delve" earning a
place says nothing about "explore". Second, one or two of these words in a
document is coincidence. Density is the signal, and density is what the corpus
study measured.

## The baseline is moving underneath the marker

`yakura-spoken-convergence` analysed over 740,000 hours of audio across 824,634
podcast episodes and 20,000 academic YouTube channels and found that words
preferentially generated by ChatGPT rose abruptly in spontaneous human speech:
delve up 48 percent, realm up 35 percent, adept up 51 percent, within 18 months
of the model's release. A pre-registered experiment with n equal to 496
confirmed entrenchment in active vocabulary.

This is not a footnote. It means the human comparison baseline that makes
excess vocabulary measurable is itself shifting toward the model distribution.
The marker is dated by construction, and the mechanism is set out in
[[Marker Cohort Rot]] and [[The Moving Baseline Objection|Human LLM Convergence]]. A 2028 recount using a
2022 baseline would measure something different from what it thinks it is
measuring.

## False positive class: who this marker wrongly flags

Naming the people this marker catches unfairly is a requirement, not a
courtesy.

1. **Second-language English writers.** Formal register learned from academic
   templates supplies exactly this vocabulary. `stowe-detector-bias` found
   English-language-learner essays disproportionately flagged by machine
   detectors, with non-White ELL students flagged disproportionately relative
   to White ELL peers, while human annotators showed no significant demographic
   bias on the same essays. See [[Detector Bias Against Language Learners]].
2. **Biomedical and grant writers.** "Crucial", "robust", "pivotal" and
   "underscore" are house vocabulary in funding prose and always have been. The
   corpus study measured a rise against their own prior baseline, which is a
   different thing from their presence.
3. **Anyone who read a lot of post-2023 text.** Convergence works on writers
   too, and the podcast finding above is direct evidence of it.
4. **Editors working from a style template.** Institutional boilerplate
   supplies the same cohort without any model involvement.
5. **Translators.** Translated academic prose reaches for high-register
   Latinate vocabulary by default.

None of these are edge cases. Together they are probably the majority of
documents this marker will fire on in ordinary use, which is precisely why it
routes rather than concludes.

## Procedure when the marker fires

1. Count cohort members per 1,000 words. Record the raw count and the words,
   never a percentage-like score.
2. If the count is at or below two distinct cohort members, stop. Record and
   take no action.
3. Above that, take each occurrence to the [[The Deletion Test|Deletion Test]]: remove the
   adjective or adverb, and state what the sentence stopped saying. Stylistic
   excess words fail this test by definition, because they carry no content.
4. For each survivor, write down the content it carried. A word that survives
   is doing work and stays.
5. Never rewrite the whole document on the strength of the count. Never report
   the count as a probability of anything.

## The complication worth keeping

`miletic-lexical-diversity` examined over 37,000 ACL Anthology papers and found
that LLM-modified text shows lower lexical diversity, which is consistent with
everything above, and that expert readers nonetheless rated the modified text
as more understandable and more exciting. Excess vocabulary is a reliable
signal of a distribution. It is not, on this evidence, a reliable signal that
the reader is worse off. That argument is developed in
[[The Accessibility Objection|Slop Is Not Always Worse]].
